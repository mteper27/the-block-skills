# NYC Land Use Counsel

Every other question — budget, design, licensing, returns — assumes the use is permitted. **That assumption is the largest binary risk in the project and it is the one most often carried as a one-line assertion in a deck.**

## Verify the as-of-right claim, do not inherit it

A statement like *"M3-1 (Heavy Manufacturing / Entertainment) — entertainment use as-of-right, most need special permits"* is a conclusion, not evidence. Get counsel to produce the actual determination in writing, and make it specific:

- Which **Use Group** does this programme fall in — and does the answer change with capacity, with dancing, with a restaurant, with an outdoor or rooftop component?
- Is that Use Group permitted **as-of-right** in this specific district, or does it require a special permit or variance?
- Does the mixed programme create **more than one** use, each with its own status? A venue plus a full restaurant plus rooftop assembly is potentially three determinations, and the weakest one governs.
- What is the existing **Certificate of Occupancy** for, and what does changing it require? A building occupied by a manufacturer has a manufacturing CofO; assembly is a different use with different everything.

Ask for the reasoning and the citations, not the conclusion. A memo you can hand a lender is worth what a verbal assurance is not.

## Price the discretionary path before you need it

If any part of the programme needs a special permit or variance, that is a **discretionary approval** with a public process: community board, borough president, city planning, and potentially council. Understand before signing:

- The realistic elapsed time — this is measured in many months to years, not weeks, and it runs *before* construction starts, on top of the build programme.
- The failure and modification risk. Discretionary approvals get granted with conditions — hours of operation, capacity caps, sound limits, loading restrictions — and those conditions can undermine the operating model that justified the deal.
- Who bears the cost and the delay if it goes badly. This belongs in the lease, not in a hope.

**A build-period rent obligation that starts before a discretionary approval is resolved is the most dangerous structure available.** Model the carry against the realistic approval timeline, not the construction timeline.

## Community and noise exposure is a land-use problem, not a PR problem

The comparable venue in this submarket failed partly on chronic permitting and community conflict, and the due-diligence list for 165 Randolph still carries *"noise / community impact"* as pending. Treat it as a design and legal input with a deadline:

- What are the applicable noise limits at the property line and at the nearest residential receptor, and what does the acoustic design have to achieve to meet them?
- What is the residential encroachment trend around the site? An M-district today is not necessarily an M-district neighbourhood in five years.
- Is there a community board relationship to build now, before an application makes it adversarial?

Acoustic isolation belongs in the construction budget as a licence-to-operate item, and it is routinely priced at zero. Land-use counsel should be the one insisting on it.

## Adjacent lots and development rights

Where the landlord controls neighbouring parcels, ask what that enables and what it exposes. Zoning-lot composition, shared access and loading, air rights and future development on adjacent sites all bear on the deal — and a landlord's plans next door can affect the venue's operations, its light and air, and its access during and after construction. Get the landlord's holdings and intentions on the record.

## Licences change; verify the current regime

Licensing regimes are amended, and a project can end up designing and budgeting around a licence that no longer exists in the form the team remembers. **New York City's cabaret law was repealed in 2017**, and what remains for a venue of this type is a different set of approvals — place of assembly, consumer-affairs licensing, and the state liquor authority. Do not carry a licence name forward from an older deck or an older project without confirming it is still the operative requirement, and do not let a design decision rest on it until counsel confirms what actually applies today.

## Never

Never let "as-of-right" into a model without a written determination behind it. Never assume one use determination covers a mixed programme. Never model a build timeline that ignores a discretionary approval sitting in front of it. Never treat community opposition as a communications workstream when it is a permitting risk.
