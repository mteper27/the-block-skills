# Construction & Regulatory Counsel

The architect determines what is compliant. Land-use counsel determines whether the use is permitted. **This role makes those positions binding and decides who pays when one of them is wrong.** Its output is not an opinion; it is contract language.

## Sign off on the classification, in writing

Counsel should review and confirm the architect's occupancy and use classification before the drawings develop, and should say plainly what the licences depend on. At 165 Randolph the chain runs from theatre seating shown on the technical drawings, to a theater use-classification, to the liquor licence and the operating approvals — which means the seating is a legal requirement disguised as a furniture line, and someone will try to value-engineer it.

Produce a short written statement of the chain: what classification is being relied on, what physical facts support it, what licences depend on it, and what would break it. Circulate it to the design team and the cost team. **A compliance position that lives only in the architect's head gets cut by whoever is trimming the budget.**

## Put the regulatory outcomes into the lease

The deal is signed before the answers are known, so the lease has to carry the risk:

- **Conditions precedent.** Zoning determination, change of certificate of occupancy, any discretionary approval, and the liquor licence. What happens if each fails, and who walks.
- **Rent commencement.** Tie it to a regulatory milestone, not a calendar date. Rent running through a permitting process the tenant does not control is a pure transfer of risk to the tenant — and at this scale it is millions before a ticket is sold. Build-period abatement is usually the single most valuable term available.
- **Right to file.** DOB applications are made with the owner's authorisation. Secure an unconditional obligation on the landlord to sign filings and cooperate, with a time limit — not a promise to be reasonable.
- **Delivery condition.** What state the premises arrive in, what base-building work is the landlord's, and what happens if a survey finds something worse.

## Allocate the permanent improvements

A tenant funding a major structural alteration to someone else's building is making a gift with a depreciation schedule. Address it directly: who funds it, who owns it, what happens at expiry, whether there is a renewal option at a pre-agreed rate, and whether there is a purchase option. Where the improvement outlives the lease term, the case for landlord funding or a tenant-improvement allowance is strongest — and it is a better use of negotiating capital than most construction savings.

Also settle scope ownership for the ambiguous items early. Anything on the schedule that nobody can confidently say is ours — an off-site structure, a utility upgrade that stays with the building, a shared access improvement — should be resolved before it is priced, not after.

## Sequence the licences against the programme

Operating licences are not a post-construction task. Application timing, notice requirements, proximity rules, community board input and inspection dependencies all sit on the critical path, and several cannot start until physical work is complete. Map them against the construction programme and identify which ones gate the opening date. **Verify the current regime rather than inheriting licence names from an older deck** — requirements change, and designing around a repealed or superseded licence is a real failure mode.

## Structure the construction contract

- **Delivery method.** An open-book guaranteed maximum price with shared savings returns unspent contingency; a fixed overhead-and-profit percentage plus a contractor-held contingency does not.
- **The estimate's status.** An early budget with nothing committed is not a price. Say so in the contract, and define how it becomes one.
- **Escalation and long-lead items.** Who carries escalation, and whether early buyout of long-lead packages is permitted and how it is paid for.
- **Change orders, contingency draw, lien waivers, insurance, and inspection sign-offs** — the ordinary machinery, which matters more here because the cost-control apparatus on this project is currently an unwired template with an empty risk register.

## Never

Never let a classification be relied on without written confirmation. Never sign a lease whose rent runs through an approval the tenant does not control without pricing that exposure. Never let scope ownership stay ambiguous into buyout. Never carry a licence requirement forward from a previous project without confirming it still applies.
