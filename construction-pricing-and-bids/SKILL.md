# Construction Pricing & Bids

Three prices for the same scope is the cheapest way ever invented to find out you were wrong. At 165 Randolph a paver line sat at **$3,312.50/SF between two lines reading $31/SF** — same product, adjacent rows — and rode the markup cascade to **$6.89M** of project total. One competing quote on that package kills it in an hour. Nobody asked for one, because nothing on the schedule was ever bid: **zero committed cost**. Competitive bidding a schedule where nothing is committed is worth 5-10% on its own, and that is before it catches anything.

So the discipline is not "get quotes". It is: **make the scope quotable, put three different kinds of seller against it, and level what comes back.**

## Accuracy is a property of the drawings, not of the estimator

Never quote a precision the design cannot support. State the estimate class and its band with every number you hand over — AACE 18R-97 is the standard worth citing, roughly:

| Class | Design definition | Typical band |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 — concept | 0-2% | −20/−50% to +30/+100% |
| 4 — study | 1-15% | −15/−30% to +20/+50% |
| 3 — budget authorisation | 10-40% | −10/−20% to +10/+30% |
| 2 — control / buyout | 30-75% | −5/−15% to +5/+20% |
| 1 — check estimate | 65-100% | −3/−10% to +3/+15% |

A Class 5 number carried to the dollar is a lie told with a spreadsheet. If someone needs ±10%, the honest answer is usually **"then we need to draw more"**, and saying so early is worth more than any sharpening of the estimate. The way to move a package up a class is to *define* it — which is the architect's half of this job, not the estimator's.

## Package the scope before you price it

You cannot level three prices for three different readings of the work. Every package gets a one-page bid form before it goes out, and every bidder gets the identical one:

- **Scope statement and drawing/spec basis** — sheet numbers and revision dates, not "per plans".
- **Quantities**, with a note on who owns the takeoff risk (lump sum vs unit price with measured re-measure).
- **Inclusions and — harder and more important — required exclusions**, so exclusions come back as a list you can price rather than a surprise at buyout.
- **What the owner or another trade supplies**: hoisting, power, water, dumpsters, patching, final clean, fire watch, temporary protection.
- **Site conditions and access** — hours, freight elevator, street permits, occupied-building constraints, union or PLA requirements. This is where the same scope becomes two different prices.
- **Schedule window and milestone dates**, because a price with no duration is not a price.
- **Commercial terms to be quoted, not assumed**: tax treatment, freight and rigging, warranty term, payment and retainage, escalation and price validity date.

A package that cannot be described this way is not ready to bid — price it as an **allowance** and label it one.

## Three quotes means three different price structures

Three bids from the same kind of firm gives you one number three times. Build the bidder list so the prices can genuinely disagree:

1. **The trade contractor route** — the sub who would carry it under the GC. Full install responsibility, carries GC markup on top.
2. **The specialist or fabricator-direct route** — the manufacturer, millworker or systems integrator, often with a separate installer. Different labour basis, different lead time.
3. **The owner-furnished or vendor-supplied route** — buy the equipment direct and have it installed, or take it against a supply agreement. Removes roughly **20% of stacked markup** on that equipment, and moves the delivery and warranty risk onto the owner. Vendor-supplied kit is never free: a distributor's coolers and draft system are priced **in margin per keg**, so get the rate quoted before anyone calls it a saving.

Where the market genuinely offers only one seller — proprietary systems, licensed or manufacturer-certified installers, a single local fabricator, a landlord-mandated contractor — say **sole source** in writing and substitute a different price test: the same vendor's price on a prior project, a first-principles build-up from labour, material and equipment hours, or a published unit rate with its source named. Three names on a page, two of which were never going to bid, is worse than an honest sole-source note.

## Run the RFQ so the three stay comparable

Same package, same due date, same bid form, one written Q&A. Every answer goes to **every** bidder as a numbered addendum, so all three price the same building. Never carry a bidder's scope suggestion into the others' packages without an addendum — that is how a bid tab quietly stops comparing anything. Never shop one bidder's number to another; besides being how you lose the good subs, the second price it produces is worthless as information.

## Level the bids

The quoted numbers are not the numbers. Build one leveling column per adjustment and show the arithmetic:

- **Add back every exclusion** at your own rate, so all three carry identical scope.
- **Deduct scope carried elsewhere**, so nothing is bought twice.
- **Price the spec delta** — an "or equal" substitution is a different product until proven otherwise.
- **Normalise the commercials**: sales tax, freight, rigging and hoisting, permits and filing fees, warranty term, escalation to the same date, payment and retainage.
- **Price the schedule**: a bid that needs six weeks longer is not the same bid.

The leveled low is often not the quoted low. **Report both**, with the delta explained line by line — that is the document that survives a buyout meeting.

## Read a low bid as a scope question

A quote materially below the other two — call it 25%+ under the median — is a missing-scope alarm, not a win, until you have interrogated it. Ask what they excluded, what quantity they took off, what spec they priced, and whether they saw the addenda. Sometimes it is a real advantage: a fabricator with the material already bought, a sub who wants the room's logo on their website. Confirm which one it is before carrying it, because the buyout premium on a bid that was wrong lands after you have committed.

The mirror case matters too: a single unsupported lump at several times the comparable budget is a **specification** failure, not a pricing one. Competitive bidding does not fix buying the wrong thing.

## What counts as a quote

Tier every price and never mix tiers inside a total without labelling them:

- **A — written quote.** Named vendor, dated, scope attached, validity period, signed or on letterhead.
- **B — budgetary or verbal.** A number a real vendor gave for planning. Usable, must be marked, must not be footed as if it were A.
- **C — desk estimate.** Built from a unit rate, a prior project, or first principles, with the basis named. This is what analysis work produces on its own.
- **D — allowance.** A placeholder for scope not yet defined. Carries the estimate class band, not a price.

**A desk estimate never wears a supplier's name.** Do not attribute a number to a vendor who did not give it, do not invent bidders to fill a three-quote table, and do not present modelled pricing as sourced. Where only Tier C is available, produce three *independent bases* instead of three vendors — the owner's own prior build, a published rate, a first-principles build-up — label the table that way, and name the live quotes still to be obtained. That is a legitimate deliverable. A fabricated bid tab is not, and it is the one error in this whole discipline that cannot be corrected later.

## Test every quote against something that is not a quote

Three quotes can agree and all be wrong — a market can be uniformly expensive, and three subs can all be pricing the same over-drawn detail. So carry an independent check on every package, normalised to a unit that travels: per LF of bar, per POS position, per seat, per capacity unit, per SF of the *right* area basis.

The strongest check available is the owner's own delivered work. `* BPT: 26 POS / Prep Kitchen / 1 Walk-in Cooler $837K` is **$32,192 per POS** delivered, against **$19,171 per POS** budgeted on the new job — a 40% variance nobody can dismiss as the wrong market. Published cost indices are the weakest check; use them only where no prior build exists, and say which one you used.

Watch the denominator. A budget reporting $313/SF on all-floor area and an underwriting reporting $607/SF on footprint describe the same building. State the basis before comparing to anything.

## Price has a shelf life

Every Tier A quote carries a validity date, and past it you hold a document, not a price. Long-lead packages — switchgear, elevators, structural steel, custom millwork, LED — should be bid and bought early against escalation, and their lead times belong in the bid tab next to the money. Escalation is applied to the whole remaining spend, deliberately: at 165 Randolph the off-site garage got 5% and the **$18.2M building got nothing**, because the line existed and was blank.

## Deliverable: the bid tab

A live sheet, one row per scope package, formulas everywhere except inputs:

`Package | Scope basis (sheet + rev) | Qty | Unit | Quote 1 / 2 / 3 (vendor, tier, date) | Exclusion add-backs | Commercial normalisation | Leveled totals | Carried number | Basis for carrying it | Unit rate check | Estimate class | Lead time | Buy-by date | Open questions`

Foot it, reconcile it to whatever budget it feeds **to the dollar**, and print the variance. Carry a number per package, never a range hidden in a comment — with the band stated at the class level, so the total's accuracy is explicit rather than implied.

## Never

Never price scope you have not packaged. Never put three prices side by side without leveling them. Never accept a low bid without its exclusion list. Never attribute a desk estimate to a vendor, and never invent a bidder to complete a table of three. Never let a quote's validity date pass unnoticed. Never carry a quote without an independent unit-rate check. Never quote a precision the drawings cannot support — say what class it is and what it would take to tighten it.
