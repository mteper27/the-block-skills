# NYC Architect — Compliance Scope

The drawings are not a description of the building. They are **the instrument that establishes what the building legally is** — and almost everything expensive downstream (licences, occupancy, opening date) is determined by decisions made in the drawing set before it is filed.

## Establish classification before anything is drawn in detail

Two separate classification systems apply and they are routinely confused:

- **Building Code occupancy group** — determines egress, fire protection, structural loading, fixture counts. Assembly splits into sub-groups by *how the space is used and whether seating is fixed*, and the sub-group drives real cost.
- **Zoning Use Group** — determines whether the use is permitted at all in this district, as-of-right or by special permit. This is a land-use question, not an architectural one; the architect draws to the answer, but the answer comes from land-use counsel. See `nyc-land-use-counsel`.

Write down which classification the operating model requires, what it demands physically, and what must appear at filing versus what can follow. Then confirm it with counsel in writing before the set develops. Retro-fitting a classification means re-drawing, re-filing and sometimes physical work in a finished building.

## The compliance items that carry cost

- **Place of Assembly.** A separate Certificate of Operation applies above a low occupant threshold; at several thousand occupants it is central, not incidental. It drives egress width and count, exit sign and emergency lighting design, and interior finish flame-spread ratings — and it is renewed, so the design must stay compliant in operation.
- **Egress.** Occupant load sets exit capacity, and a mezzanine adds occupants without adding a footprint. Egress stair count, width, discharge, and travel distance are what actually cap the room's capacity — not floor area.
- **Plumbing fixture counts.** Set by occupant load and by occupancy group. This is one of the few places where a budget can be checked objectively: at 165 Randolph the development budget carried 189 fixtures against an underwriting assumption of 100, and the development budget was right.
- **Accessibility.** Accessible route to every level the public reaches, accessible seating dispersed rather than clustered, companion seating, and vertical circulation. A single two-stop hydraulic lift does not serve a four-level venue.
- **Interior finishes and acoustic treatment.** Flame-spread classification applies to acoustic panels and drape. Specify to the rating, because a treatment that fails it gets pulled out after installation.

## The filing path for a major alteration

A roof raise plus a new mezzanine is a major alteration, not a fit-out. Expect a structural filing with its own supporting work: geotechnical borings, foundation design, special and progress inspections, and controlled-inspection sign-offs at each stage. The geotechnical scope is a leading indicator — a budget that funds *"13 borings 50 ft deep required for the structural permit"* is telling you the structural filing is real, and any budget that funds the borings while pricing the structural work at zero has a gap.

Sequence matters more than duration. Structural approval, FDNY plan approval, elevator filings and utility service approvals run partly in parallel and partly in series, and a single dependency can gate a Temporary Certificate of Occupancy. Ask the architect for the **filing critical path**, not a fee proposal, and drive the construction programme from it.

## FDNY

Separate submissions from DOB with their own review: fire protection plan, fire alarm and smoke detection filing and inspection, fire safety and emergency action plan, and place-of-assembly related approvals. These are frequently the last thing to clear before opening and the first thing forgotten in a soft-cost budget. Carry them as explicit lines.

## Testing the test fit

A test fit is an argument about capacity, and it should be checked against the model it is used to justify:

- Does the seated and standing capacity reconcile to the egress capacity, or only to floor area at an assumed SF-per-occupant?
- Does the furniture schedule match the drawn capacity? At 165 Randolph the model assumed 1,400 mezzanine seats while the FF&E schedule bought 720 chairs, and assumed 144 box seats against 657 box chairs purchased.
- Is the programme in the fit — restaurant, VIP, roof decks — actually wanted, or is it inherited? Scope that traces to the test fit is a design decision to revisit with the architect, and it is not a contractor pricing problem.

## Never

Never let classification be settled by the person pricing the furniture. Never assume floor area sets capacity — egress does. Never treat FDNY as a subset of the DOB filing. Never value-engineer a fixture count, an egress width or seating that a classification depends on; reprice it instead.
