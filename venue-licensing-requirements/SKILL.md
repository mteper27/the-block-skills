# Venue Licensing Requirements

The licences are not paperwork that happens after construction. They are **physical requirements on the drawings**, and the cheapest moment to satisfy them is before filing. Value-engineering that ignores them can delete the thing the licence depends on.

## The dependency chain

At 165 Randolph it runs: **theatre seating on the technical drawings → theater use-classification → liquor licence and cabaret licence.** Cut the seating to save furniture money and the classification fails, and with it the licences that make the room a business. This chain is the first thing to establish on any venue project, because it determines which scope is genuinely optional.

Establish it explicitly and in writing, with counsel:

- What classification does the operating model require?
- What does that classification demand physically — seat count, fixed versus removable, which floor, sightlines, aisles, egress?
- What must appear on the drawings at filing, as opposed to what can be added later?
- Which licence depends on which classification, and what happens to the others if one fails?

## Separate the compliance floor from the aesthetic choice

Once the requirement is known, price at least three levels. The spread is usually large and it is almost always mistaken for one decision:

- **Compliance** — the minimum that satisfies the classification. Removable seating on one level, shown on the drawings, stacked into storage on other nights. Add seat and row numbering, aisle marking and storage; these are real costs that compliance-minimum schemes forget.
- **As budgeted** — whatever the current drawing set implies, which is frequently neither the cheapest compliant answer nor a good version of the real thing.
- **The full treatment** — fixed upholstered seating on raked tiering with designed sightlines. A different building.

At 165 Randolph those priced at roughly **$74K / $801K / $3.23M**. The middle option — the one in the budget — seated the floor at $125 a chair: more than compliance required and nothing like a theater.

**Reprice before you delete.** The right move on the compliance option was not removing seats but sourcing them at $25 rather than $125 — same compliance, $371K saved. Always test price before scope when the scope is load-bearing for a licence.

## Carry the soft costs

Licensing soft costs are routinely filed at zero because they get folded into a due-diligence line. They are not zero. Carry explicit lines for the classification filing, liquor licence legal, cabaret licence, fire-safety and emergency action plans, fire-protection plan filing, and the expediting that goes with them. If a budget shows attorney fees at $0 with a note that they sit inside a feasibility study, treat that as unpriced.

## Sequence

Classification is set at filing. Retro-fitting it means re-filing, re-drawing and sometimes physical work in a finished building — and it can simply fail. **Get the legal answer before anyone prices a chair**, and put the seat count in a single input cell so every downstream number moves with it when counsel answers.

## Never

Never let a seat count in a model stand as a legal conclusion — flag it as a placeholder until counsel confirms it. Never value-engineer seating, egress or fixture counts without checking the classification first. Never treat licensing as a post-construction workstream.
