# Venue Equipment Sourcing

The goal is not the cheapest equipment. It is **the least money that delivers what the room has to do** — which usually means paying up on the few things that earn and stripping the many that do not.

## Benchmark against your own builds first

Published cost indices are worth less than one prior project by the same owner. Estimators leave those benchmarks in the notes column: a line reading `* BPT: 26 POS / Prep Kitchen / 1 Walk-in Cooler $837K` is a delivered build at **$32,192 per POS**, against $19,171 per POS budgeted on the new job. That 40% variance against your own delivered project is the strongest argument available in either direction, and nobody can dismiss it as the wrong market.

Normalise to a unit that travels: per POS position, per seat, per capacity unit, per SF of bar. Compare on that, not on package totals.

## Bars: built-in vs container vs modular

Run this as a real comparison, not a preference:

- **Built-in millwork** — roughly $900/LF plus back bar. Permanent, best throughput per LF, highest finish, and it fixes the room's layout for good. Carries plumbing and electrical roughing per bar ($40K-75K each) that is easy to forget and is *never* optional.
- **Container or modular** — lower capital, relocatable, and it lets bar count follow demand as the calendar proves out. Weaker at high throughput, needs quick-connect services designed in from day one, and reads as temporary unless it is deliberately part of the aesthetic. In a raw-industrial room it usually is.
- **Vendor-supplied** — coolers, draft systems and ice funded by a distributor against a pouring agreement. Zero capital. **Priced in margin per keg, not cash** — get that rate quoted before signing, because it is a permanent cost against a one-time saving.

Bar *count* is the bigger lever than bar *type*: dropping nine bars to six on a lean concept took roughly $520K out across millwork, equipment and roughing at 165 Randolph. Size the count from expected throughput at peak, not from the floor plan's convenient locations.

## Sound and production: buy, lease or rent

Do the payback arithmetic explicitly, because intuition is reliably wrong here. Measure the rental alternative from the operator's own settled shows, not from a rate card.

At Factory Town the per-run rental lines that ownership eliminates — lighting, audio, rigging, lift, comms — run about **$135,500 per run**. Against a ~$3.05M owned package that is a payback of **2 to 7 months** across any plausible calendar. The conclusion generalises: for a room running a real calendar, **event-renting the core PA and lighting is the most expensive option available.**

So separate the three mechanisms and use each for what it is good at:

- **Equipment lease** (fixed term, own at the end) — the right way to defer capital on core systems you will use constantly. Cheaper than renting, keeps the asset.
- **Event rental** — correct for what is occasional or dates fast. LED walls are the clearest example: spec moves quickly, they are genuinely modular, and they carry no later-premium for being deferred — provided the rigging or ground support to hang them exists from day one.
- **Vendor-supplied or sponsored** — POS, WiFi and CCTV are routinely supplied free or subsidised against exclusivity or venue use. Take it, and price the exclusivity.

**Financing does not make the building cheaper, only cheaper to start.** Say so plainly whenever it is proposed as a saving.

## Ground-supported beats hung, when it is available

If the stage and production towers stand on the slab rather than hanging from the building, the roof stops needing rigging capacity. That removes heavy transfer girders and reinforced point-load nodes, and drops roof steel from roughly 20 lb/SF to 15. It is a production decision with a structural payoff an order of magnitude larger than the equipment cost — always test it before pricing a rigging grid.

## Outsource whole scopes, not just items

A kitchen is the clearest case. Handing it to an operator removes the equipment, the plumbing roughing, the gas run, hood fire suppression and grease waste — and the operator funds their own fit-out. What stays yours is the bars, always. When outsourcing, write down the two conditions that get forgotten: which services still have to be roughed in by us, and what the arrangement does to the revenue model.

## Specify against the job, not the category

Ask what each item must do before pricing it. A single unsupported architectural lighting lump at 3.4x the performance-lighting budget, in a room whose entire proposition is production, is a specification failure rather than a pricing one — no amount of competitive bidding fixes buying the wrong thing.

## Never

Never compare package totals; normalise to a unit. Never accept a rental proposal without the payback arithmetic from the operator's own shows. Never delete equipment that a licence or a classification depends on — reprice it instead. Never let vendor-supplied kit into a budget as free without the margin rate attached.
